# Report — findings and decision framework

Every table, and every number inside a `BEGIN`/`END` block, is generated by `make report` from
`results/runs/*.json` and `results/quality/*.json`; rebuilding on unchanged results leaves
`git diff` empty. The prose between the blocks quotes some of those numbers and is checked
against them at each release. See [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md) for how the measurements are
produced and which of them are not trustworthy.

**What was measured.** Every run below is on one NVIDIA A40 (sm_86) serving
`meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct` at `max_model_len=4096`: open-loop Poisson arrivals seeded
identically across configurations, clocks locked (recorded with every run; from v0.2.3 also
re-checked against the clocks sampled during it), kernel selection asserted from engine startup
logs, plus the runs that test the method itself (§6).

<!-- BEGIN:headline -->
**266 measured runs** across 6 configurations, 6 quality evaluations, open-loop, at least three repeats per point.

Under a **p95 TTFT budget of 500 ms**, `vllm-int8-w8a8` is cost-optimal: **2116 output tokens/sec at $0.0525 per million tokens** — 27 % cheaper than `vllm-bf16`. `vllm-bf16` is dominated: 4 configurations are cheaper at equal-or-better measured quality.

The grey band on the chart is `vllm-bf16`'s 95 % confidence interval on GSM8K, and every configuration falls inside it. The quality spread across all 6 configurations is 0.0159, smaller than a single configuration's 95 % half-width of 0.0240 — **quality does not separate these configurations at this sample size**, so the decision is made on cost and latency.

## 2. Finding 1 — decode time tracks weight bytes, not FLOPs

The premise of the whole quantization axis was that A40 decode is memory-bandwidth-bound. If
that is true, shrinking the weights should speed up decode in proportion to the bytes no longer
read. Measured at 1 req/s, where batching is minimal and the bandwidth bound is cleanest:

<!-- BEGIN:bandwidth-table -->
| Config | Declared weights | Predicted speedup (byte ratio) | Measured TPOT p95 | Measured speedup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `vllm-bf16` | 15.00 GiB | 1.00x | 32.56 ms | 1.00x |
| `vllm-int8-w8a8` | 8.50 GiB | 1.76x | 18.59 ms | **1.75x** |
| `vllm-gptq-int4` | 5.70 GiB | 2.63x | 10.96 ms | 2.97x |
| `vllm-awq-int4` | 5.70 GiB | 2.63x | 10.33 ms | 3.15x |

Act-order overhead, isolated: `gptq_marlin` 10.96 ms vs `awq_marlin` 10.33 ms — **a 6.2 % decode penalty** for `desc_act=true` at identical bit width.
<!-- END:bandwidth-table -->

**INT8 lands on the prediction within about 1 %.** That is the cleanest test in the table, because
W8A8 changes weight *and* activation precision together and leaves no dequantization step to
confound the timing. Decode on this GPU is bandwidth-bound, and the bytes-read model predicts
it quantitatively rather than directionally.

**The INT4 configurations beat the prediction, and the bandwidth model does not explain that.**
A pure bytes-read model cannot produce a speedup larger than the byte ratio — any fixed
per-token overhead pushes the measured ratio *below* it, not above. The likely cause is that
the baseline is not itself bandwidth-optimal: Marlin is specifically tuned for the low-batch
regime, while vLLM's general BF16 GEMM path is not, so part of the INT4 speedup beyond 2.63× is
kernel quality rather than byte count. **This benchmark does not isolate that**, and the number is reported as
unexplained rather than claimed as confirmation.

### Act-order overhead, isolated

Both INT4 checkpoints are 4-bit, group size 128, and both select a Marlin kernel. They differ
in that the GPTQ checkpoint carries `desc_act=true`, which `gptq_marlin` handles with a
load-time permutation; AWQ-GEMM has no act-order concept. The pair therefore isolates that
overhead rather than duplicating a data point — the penalty is the last line of the table
above. Under saturation it compounds rather than staying constant (§3).

## 3. Finding 2 — the best quantization inverts between load regimes

This is the result that would be missed by any benchmark reporting a single throughput number.

<!-- BEGIN:regime-table -->
| Offered rate | TPOT p95 winner | TTFT p95 winner | Configs still valid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 rps | `vllm-awq-int4` (10.3 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (97 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 2 rps | `vllm-awq-int4` (11.5 ms) | `vllm-awq-int4` (87 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 4 rps | `vllm-awq-int4` (16.2 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (160 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 5 rps | `vllm-awq-int4` (19.9 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (208 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 6 rps | `vllm-awq-int4` (25.4 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (276 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 7 rps | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (47.4 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (330 ms) | 4 of 4 |
| 8 rps | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (66.7 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (459 ms) | 3 of 4 |
| 9 rps | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (102.0 ms) | `vllm-int8-w8a8` (2353 ms) | 1 of 4 |
<!-- END:regime-table -->

INT4 holds the decode advantage through 6 rps. At 7 rps INT8 overtakes it on decode as well as
prefill, and at 8 req/s the INT4 configurations do not merely lose — they collapse. TTFT p95
goes to **3288 ms for AWQ and 9594 ms for GPTQ, against 459 ms for INT8**, a 7× and 21× gap
against a configuration that was *behind* on decode latency at every rate below the knee. BF16 does not reach
8 rps at all; every repeat was classified `oversubscribed`.

**The mechanism is the activation precision, not the weight precision.** Both INT4 formats here
are W4A16: 4-bit weights, 16-bit activations. The GEMM dequantizes weights to FP16 and does
FP16 math, so INT4 buys bandwidth and nothing else. That is exactly right for decode, which is
bandwidth-bound — and worth nothing for prefill, which is compute-bound, where it additionally
pays for the dequantization. W8A8 is different in kind: it feeds the A40's INT8 tensor cores
(~299 TOPS against ~149.7 TFLOPS BF16), so it accelerates prefill *arithmetic*. As offered load
rises, prefill work grows, the bottleneck migrates from bandwidth to compute, and the
configuration that only ever addressed bandwidth runs out of room.

GPTQ collapsing harder than AWQ (9594 vs 3288 ms) is the act-order penalty compounding: a
6.2 % per-token cost at low load becomes a queueing catastrophe once the server is at capacity,
because the service rate falls below the arrival rate and the queue grows without bound.

**Practical consequence.** "Which quantization is fastest" has no load-independent answer on
this hardware. Below the knee, pick for decode bandwidth: INT4. At or near the knee, pick for
prefill compute: INT8. A benchmark that reports one rate cannot see this.

## 4. Decision framework — cost-optimal configuration vs SLA

Given a p95 TTFT budget, the cheapest configuration that meets it:

<!-- BEGIN:sla-table -->
| p95 TTFT budget | Cheapest config | Rate | Throughput | $/1M tokens | Admissible |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 ms | `vllm-awq-int4` | 4 rps | 1112 tok/s | $0.0999 | 6 of 6 |
| 500 ms | `vllm-int8-w8a8` | 8 rps | 2116 tok/s | $0.0525 | 6 of 6 |
| 1000 ms | `vllm-int8-w8a8` | 8 rps | 2116 tok/s | $0.0525 | 6 of 6 |
| 2000 ms | `vllm-int8-w8a8` | 8 rps | 2116 tok/s | $0.0525 | 6 of 6 |
<!-- END:sla-table -->

The crossover at the tight end is real: at a 200 ms budget no configuration sustains its
highest rate, so the ranking is decided by which one still meets the budget at 4 rps, and
`vllm-awq-int4` wins on cost there. From 500 ms upward `vllm-int8-w8a8` at 8 rps wins, and the
answer stops changing — not because the budget stops mattering, but because INT8 has nothing
further to offer: at 9 rps it is still valid, but its queue grows through the run and its p95
TTFT is past even the loosest budget in the table, and from 10 rps it is `oversubscribed`.
**Above roughly 500 ms the binding constraint is engine capacity, not the SLA**, and a
further-loosened budget buys nothing.

### How this transfers off the A40

The mechanism generalizes even though the numbers do not. The question to ask of any
accelerator is where its arithmetic intensity sits relative to the model's: the A40 is
compute-rich and bandwidth-poor (~215 FLOP/byte against ~153 for an A100-80GB), which is why
weight-only quantization pays here more than published A100 results suggest, and why the
prefill regime flips to favouring a format with real low-precision compute. On a part with FP8
tensor cores (sm_89/sm_90) the W8A8 role would be filled by FP8 rather than INT8 — which is
precisely why FP8 was excluded here rather than faked (§7).

## 6. Does the methodology hold up?

The rest of this report rests on three methodological claims. Each is testable,
and each has a run whose only purpose is to test it rather than to rank a configuration.
None of these appear on the frontier: a trace replay and a closed-loop run carry no offered
rate, which is the field every aggregation keys on, so they are structurally excluded from
the operating points above.

### The arrival process — what a closed loop gets wrong, and what Poisson costs

Both exhibits face the same server and the same prompts as the open-loop baseline; only the
arrival process differs. The closed-loop pool size is swept so that a throughput-matched point
exists for each open-loop rate, because a tail comparison across different loads measures the
load, not the generator. The trace replay is designed as several independent, non-overlapping
windows against as many independent Poisson seeds, interleaved, because one seeded schedule
repeated three times measures the server's variability rather than the arrival process's.
The first arrival study did not achieve that design: a bug in window selection made all five
trace replays the same window. It was re-run with the fix: five non-overlapping windows of the
trace, each time-scaled to exactly 4 rps (factors 1.00–1.11, which leave CV² unchanged; the
windows' CV² runs from 0.99 to 1.61), interleaved with five fresh Poisson draws. That study is
the trace row below; the earlier runs remain in `results/runs` as history.

<!-- BEGIN:methodology-table -->
| Arrival process | Matched to | TTFT p50 | TTFT p95 | TTFT p99 | Throughput | Tail vs open-loop Poisson |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Open-loop Poisson** (baseline) | 4 rps | 155 ms | 303 ms | 521 ms | 1082 tok/s | — |
| Azure trace replay | 4 rps | 159 ms | 318 ms | 545 ms | 1102 tok/s | indistinguishable (+24 ms against ±36 ms) |
| Closed loop (N=8) | 1 rps (250 ms p99) | 91 ms | 199 ms | 402 ms | 252 tok/s | **1.6x worse** (p99 +151 ms) |

| Closed loop (N=160) | 7 rps (988 ms p99) | 315 ms | 643 ms | 828 ms | 1692 tok/s | **1.19x understated** (p99 -159 ms) |
<!-- END:methodology-table -->

**The closed loop's error changes sign with load — and its first measurement was wrong.** The
textbook objection to closed-loop generators is *coordinated omission*: when the server stalls, a
generator that waits for completions stops sampling, so the slow periods are under-represented
and the reported tail is optimistic. That was the claim here, and it predicted that the
closed-loop rows would *understate* the open-loop p99.

The first measurement appeared to refute it: near the knee the closed loop reported a p99 three
to four times *worse*. That was an artefact of the harness. All N workers start at the same
instant, and with N above the 50-request warm-up (N = 64 to 160) the tail of that start-up burst
was in the measured sample. Re-measured with a warm-up of at least N, and with per-request
records kept, the burst requests alone reach 2.2–4.0 s — exactly where the old p99 sat — while
the measured requests do not. Near the knee the closed loop now does what the textbook says: it
understates the open-loop p99, modestly (the table). At 4 rps the two generators are
indistinguishable.

At light load the sign genuinely reverses: at 1 rps the closed loop's p99 is 1.6× worse, with no
burst in its sample. The proposed mechanism is occupancy. A continuous-batching engine below
saturation has no stalls for coordinated omission to hide; what a closed loop does instead is hold
occupancy at its maximum, so every new request's prefill competes with a full batch of decodes,
while Poisson arrivals at the same mean let occupancy fluctuate and requests that land in a lull
get fast prefill. Near capacity the server does stall, and the textbook effect takes over.

Open-loop remains the correct methodology, for the one reason that holds at every load: a closed
loop's offered load is a *consequence of the server's speed*, so it cannot measure the server
against any load a real deployment would actually receive. Pessimistic at light load, optimistic
near capacity — an error that changes sign with the thing being measured is what a generator
coupled to its subject produces.

The re-measured near-knee rows ran on GPU 1, the same card as their open-loop baseline; the first
measurement's N = 96 and 128 rows had run on GPU 0. Those first runs are kept in `results/runs`
as history (`vllm-bf16-closed-loop-warmup50__*`); the comparison uses the latest session.

**At this rate the Poisson assumption costs nothing measurable.** Across five distinct windows
of real traffic, the trace's p99 sits inside the interval shown in the table. The one clearly
bursty window (CV² 1.61) produced the highest p99 of all ten runs, so burstier traffic may well
cost the tail something; five windows cannot resolve an effect of that size, and on average
there is none to see.

**Below saturation the engines deliver the same throughput.** At 1 and 4 rps the same checkpoint
delivers the same throughput on either engine to within 3 %, and TTFT differs by up to about
40 ms in either direction. Whatever the quantization findings above are, they are not vLLM
artifacts. Decode is the exception: from 5 rps `sglang-awq-int4`'s p95 TPOT is 1.6–2.4× vLLM's
on the same checkpoint and kernel (latency table, §5).

**At saturation SGLang degrades more gracefully.** At 8 rps `sglang-bf16` remains reportable
where `vllm-bf16` is oversubscribed in every repeat, and `sglang-awq-int4` holds a far lower
TTFT tail than vLLM on the same checkpoint and kernel. This is a scheduling and admission
difference, not a kernel difference — both engines select `awq_marlin`, asserted from their
startup logs. It was not investigated further and is reported as an observation.

**A confound applies to this section specifically.** Both SGLang configurations were measured
with a busy neighbour GPU in the same chassis, while most of the vLLM ladder was not. That
stamp travels with every affected record. Its measured effect across the 209 valid runs is
1.5 °C *cooler* at essentially the same mean SM clock (within 3 MHz) for busy-neighbour runs, with zero
throttled samples anywhere — the opposite direction to the feared effect, and small. The comparison is reported
with the asterisk rather than without it.

## 8. Limitations

- **One model, one GPU, one context length.** Llama-3.1-8B at `max_model_len=4096` on a single
  A40. Nothing here establishes behaviour at 70B, at long context, or across tensor parallelism.
- **The INT4 decode speedup exceeds what the bandwidth model predicts** and this benchmark does
  not isolate why (§2). The INT8 result, which does fit, is the load-bearing evidence.
- **The quality axis is unresolved.** All six configurations lie inside one another's
  confidence intervals on both task benchmarks (§5). Perplexity separates them; task accuracy
  does not, and the Pareto frontier's quality ordering should not be read as significant.
- **SGLang has no perplexity.** GSM8K and IFEval were measured through SGLang and match vLLM
  on the same checkpoint (0.7316 / 0.7316), but scoring a prompt needs `echo=true` with
  `max_tokens=0`, a vLLM extension SGLang rejects with a 400. SGLang is on the frontier by task
  score; its perplexity column is a gap rather than a guess.
- **FP8 is absent by design, not by omission.** The A40 is sm_86 and has no FP8 tensor cores.
  vLLM will still *load* an FP8 checkpoint on Ampere by dequantizing to FP16 — it appears to
  work, yields weight-only compression, and delivers no compute speedup. Publishing that as
  "FP8 on A40" is the error class this repository exists to avoid.
- **`ignore_eos=True`** forces every configuration to perform identical work, which is what
  makes the comparison fair and simultaneously makes the output lengths less realistic than the
  input lengths. The tradeoff is deliberate and stated in METHODOLOGY.md §2.
- **INT8's ceiling is between 9 and 10 rps.** 9 rps is valid at ~2300 tok/s, but its queue
  grows through the run (p95 TTFT above 2 s), so it is not a sustainable operating point; 10 and
  11 rps are oversubscribed. The ladder was extended for INT8 alone because it was the only
  configuration still healthy at 8 rps.
- **Valid is not the same as steady.** The validity guard labels a run oversubscribed only when
  its achieved rate falls below 80 % of the offered one, measured over a window that includes the
  drain after the last request. The highest valid rate of INT4 (8 rps) and INT8 (9 rps) are
  runs whose queue was still growing. From v0.2.3 such runs carry a note saying so.
- **The closed loop's error changes sign with load** (§6). Its first measurement, a
  three-to-four-fold *worse* tail at the knee, was a harness artefact and was re-measured. The
  light-load effect is real, but its occupancy mechanism is proposed, not isolated.
- **The arrival study covers one trace at one rate.** Five windows of the Azure conversation
  trace at 4 rps (§6); other traces, and rates nearer the knee, were not measured.
- **The cost figure is a public reference price, not an invoice.** The measurement GPU is a lab
  machine. Because every configuration runs on the same GPU, the price is a linear scalar and
  cannot reorder the ranking — which is why no price-sensitivity table appears here.
